One week, six releases: an AI car-discovery agent

Car search works when shoppers type filters and breaks when they talk like people. I spent a week finding out why, fixing the data underneath, building an agent on top, and testing it on real buyers, the way I'd run it as the product manager.

In 60 seconds

The story in 60 seconds

I typed 20 real shopper requests into a major used-car site. Phrased like filters, 95% of the top results fit. Phrased like a person, only 37% did, and "not a minivan" returned minivans.

Many failures were data, not language: no dealer feed sent tow ratings or horsepower. So I built a SQL pipeline that turned 720 messy listings, none usable by an agent, into 467 that were. Then I built the agent: the language model only reads the request, and plain code finds, ranks and explains the cars, so it can't invent a spec.

It scored 100% on my own test, and I didn't trust that. Digging in, I found hidden problems, and after the fixes it scored 91%, against the site's 37%. Then six real buyers caught what my test couldn't. Four of them couldn't find a monthly payment online, so I built payment search, without ever asking about income or credit.

Step by step

The week

v0.1Oct 25 · Research

Listen to shoppers, then test the search

What I didCoded 14 real shopper letters. Typed 20 shopper-style searches into a major used-car site and scored the top 5. Wrote a PR/FAQ.
What I found13 of 14 started from a need, not a model. Filter-style searches: 95% fit; people-style: 37%. "Not a minivan" added a minivan filter, and "$400 a month" silently became a sticker price.
What I decidedMany failures were missing data, not misread words. Fix the data before building any AI.
v0.2Oct 25 · Data

Feed Doctor: make the inventory answerable

What I didBuilt a DuckDB SQL pipeline over four messy dealer feeds: normalize, flag issues, de-duplicate, add specs from a trim reference. Tested it on planted problems and on an unseen dealer (a held-out test).
What I found0 of 720 listings were agent-ready as sent; 454 after. It caught 331 of 333 planted issues, but only 32 of 87 on the unseen dealer, where a $34,895 car became $3,489,500.
What I decidedEvery new feed needs its own quality gate. Drivetrain needs VIN decoding, not more text rules.
v0.2.1Oct 28 · Data

Sanity gates

What I didFixed parsing (cents, "13.0k mi", dates) and added gates that hold back impossible prices, years and mileages.
What I foundThe unseen dealer: 85 of 87 handled, no bad listings published. One genuine 2024 car with 800 miles was held back too.
What I decidedGates trade a little recall for safety, so in production gated listings go to a review queue, not the bin.
v0.3Oct 28 · Agent

The agent: the model reads, the code decides

What I didA language model fills a strict intent schema; plain code filters, loosens when nothing matches, ranks and writes "why this car" from listing facts. Filters show as removable chips.
What I foundKeeping the model out of the decisions made every result explainable and every bug findable.
What I decidedSame trust pattern as my Toyota agent: put AI where it's strong, keep a checkable step where the risk is.
v0.3.1–2Oct 29 · Evaluate

A perfect score I didn't trust

What I didRan the 20 v0.1 searches through the agent live and read every row, not just the score.
What I found100% hid real problems: "fun" became "250+ hp", invented filters, hidden cars. After fixes it fell to 91%, because my own new rule sent minivans to a shopper who wanted easy parking.
What I decidedStop tuning on a test I wrote; it had become a practice set. Get searches from real buyers instead.
v0.4Oct 31 · Real buyers

Six buyers, their words, their answer keys

What I didInterviewed 6 people shopping for a car. Turned their words into a held-out test, scored on what they said they'd consider, and ran it once, unseen.
What I found86% fit, but it asked the question buyers wanted only 1 time in 4: my "fewer questions" fix had overshot. And 4 of 6 couldn't find a monthly payment online.
What I decidedAlways ask about budget when none is given. Make payment the next release.
v0.5–0.6Oct 31 · Ship

What buyers asked for, built responsibly

What I didPayments shoppers control (down, trade-in, term, rate), "what can I afford?", base-trim preference, a compare view, and 14 automated tests on every change.
What I foundOne buyer wanted an income-based check. A lender's search shouldn't infer anyone's finances.
What I decidedThe shopper picks a payment; RightFit never asks about income or credit. Ranking uses only what they asked for, and a test proves it.
What I'd bring to the next team

What I learned

  1. Start from how people talk, not how the database is shaped. 13 of 14 letter-writers and most buyers described a need, not a model. A search built for filters serves the minority.
  2. Many AI failures are data failures. No language model can find "a truck that tows 7,000 lb" if no listing has a tow rating. The agent and the inventory have to be built together.
  3. A perfect score is a warning sign. 100% hid invented filters and hidden cars. I read rows, not just totals, and made the evaluation check what the score couldn't see.
  4. Your own test becomes a practice set; real users catch what you can't. Six buyers found the gap my 20 searches never would: they wanted to be asked about budget.
  5. Put the model where it's strong and keep decisions checkable. The model reads; code decides, explains and ranks fairly, with no paid placement, no inferred income and a test that proves it.

Honest limits

  • The inventory is synthetic and the specs are illustrative.
  • Samples are small: 14 letters, 20 audit searches, 6 buyers.
  • I wrote most of the tests; the 6 buyer searches have now been seen too.
  • The retailer audit is one site on one day.

If it went to production next

  • Real dealer feeds with VIN decoding for drivetrain and trim
  • CarPlay, navigation and EV-charging data
  • Availability at the nearest dealer
  • Real lender rates, with a soft-pull pre-qualification the shopper opts into
  • New buyers for the next held-out test
