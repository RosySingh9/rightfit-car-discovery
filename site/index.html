Can a shopper describe their life in one sentence and find the right car?

Vehicle search works when shoppers talk in filters. It breaks when they talk like people.

RightFit is an AI discovery agent I'm building in public, release by release. Release v0.1 looks at how real shoppers describe what they want, and tests whether a major retailer's search box understands them.

When the shopper already speaks in filters, search is excellent.

Two kinds of failure. Some are about language: negation, comparisons, vague needs, thresholds. Many are about data: horsepower, tow rating, EV range and driver-assist features weren't filterable, and some body types were wrong. An AI agent can't reason over facts the inventory doesn't hold, so the agent and the inventory data have to be built together.

What I'm building

The agent

  1. Understand. Turn one message into a structured intent: constraints, exclusions, preferences, context.
  2. Ask, don't guess. One clarifying question when the intent is vague or the results would be empty.
  3. Clean the data. Normalize and enrich dealer feeds so tow rating, range, features and body type are facts the agent can use.
  4. Find and explain. Filters and facets, then ranking, then a one-line "why this car" built only from listing facts.
  5. Prove it. These 20 queries become a gold set, scored on every change and published as a scoreboard.

PR/FAQ: RightFit

Working-backwards draft · figures are hypotheses

Shoppers describe their life, not a spec sheet, and see the cars that fit, each with a monthly payment whose assumptions are in plain view.

Why the lender should build it

Payment is how most people actually budget. The lender is the one party that can show an honest, explained payment estimate next to every result, and it has a reason to care about trust and compliance from the start.

How success is measured
  • North star: findability rate, the share of sessions that reach a vehicle page matching every hard constraint the shopper stated.
  • Leading: zero-result rate, rephrase rate, time to first relevant result.
  • Quality: constraints honored, negation accuracy, top-5 precision on a gold set, and invented specs in explanations (target: zero).
  • Business: vehicle page to lead, and to credit application, against a control group.
  • Data: attribute completeness and accuracy per feed, listing freshness.
Guardrails
  • Ranking and payment estimates never use protected characteristics or proxies for them, and the agent never infers creditworthiness.
  • Every applied filter shows as a chip the shopper can remove.
  • Explanations use listing facts only; evals check this before each release.
  • Quality bars are agreed with the business owner, and launch waits until they're met.

Method and limits

Each query was typed exactly as written into the site's public search box, and the applied filters, result count and top 5 were recorded. Top-5 fit was judged by the author against public specs; two queries that couldn't be verified are left out of the fit score. One main site, one day, one location, a small sample. Cars.com showed a bot check and wasn't tested. On Toyota.com's public new-inventory search, the entry point is a grid of models with no free-text box on that page. That's one observation of one public page, and it says nothing about any internal or upcoming product. All queries, raw results and scoring are in the repo.
