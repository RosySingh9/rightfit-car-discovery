Can a shopper describe their life in one sentence and find the right car?

Vehicle search works when shoppers talk in filters. It breaks when they talk like people.

RightFit is an AI car-discovery agent I built release by release: research on how shoppers talk, an inventory data pipeline, the agent, honest evaluations against a major retailer's search, a test on six real buyers, and the finance-aware search those buyers asked for.

How it works
  • Understand: a language model (GPT-4o-mini) turns your sentence into structured filters, exclusions and preferences. That's all it does.
  • Find: plain code filters and ranks the cleaned inventory from v0.2. The model never picks cars or states specs.
  • Explain: each "why" line is built only from the listing's own data, so it can't invent a feature.
  • Be honest: if nothing matches, it says which requirement it loosened. If you ask for something the data can't check, like leather seats, it says so.
  • Payments you control: every car shows an estimated monthly payment. Change the down payment, trade-in, term or rate, or enter a payment you're comfortable with to see what it covers. RightFit never asks about or guesses income or credit.
  • Compare: tick up to three cars to see them side by side, with the best value in each row marked.
  • Fair order: results are ranked only by what you asked for and each car's own data, never by paid placement or anything about you. The sort order is shown above the results, and an automated test checks it on every change.
  • Measured: the 20 shopper queries from v0.1 and 6 real-buyer searches run through the agent and are scored live. Run the evaluation.

What the buyers caught

  • My own fix had overshot. The practice set taught the agent to ask fewer questions. Real buyers wanted the budget question, and it asked 1 time in 4.
  • "No fancy add-ons" became a requirement for adaptive cruise control, which is an add-on. The top 5 were all Subarus, with none of the RAV4, Camry or Pilot that buyer was considering.
  • "Safe" on a $20k budget required three safety features. That hid the Camry, Corolla and Civic the buyer would have taken, leaving one car.

The biggest finding isn't a bug

4 of 6 buyers couldn't work out a monthly payment or what they could afford online. One only got a payment at the dealer. That makes a finance-aware search the top priority: v0.5 adds search by monthly payment with editable assumptions, and a "what can I afford?" helper where the shopper enters their own numbers. Why run 1's 100% was wrong

I wrote the agent, the tests and the inventory, so top-5 fit mostly measured parsing. Reading every row showed filters nobody asked for ("fun" became 250+ hp, which left an AWD sedan search with zero cars), unneeded questions, and 3 EVs hidden by a filter that listed every drivetrain. Run 2 fixed nearly all of it.

Why run 2 dropped to 91%

One of my fixes backfired. A new rule, "roomy means rank by cargo space", sent five Sienna minivans to the top for a shopper who wanted "a quiet hybrid that fits a stroller and is easy to park". The score caught it. v0.3.2 treats "fits a stroller" as a minimum, not a ranking, and reads "easy to park" as no minivans or pickups.

What I'm not doing: tuning until these 20 searches score 100% again. After two rounds of fixes they've become a practice set. v0.4 tests on searches I didn't write, taken from buyer interviews. Raw results: run 1 · run 2. Run it yourself.

Drivetrain is the blocker

The marketplace feed doesn't send drivetrain, and dealers bury it in trim names like "XLE AWD-i". At 82%, four of the twenty shopper queries stay unanswerable: AWD sedan, AWD wagon, 4x4 for camping, AWD minivan. The fix is decoding the VIN, not more text rules.

99% on what I planned for. 37% on what I didn't.

The pipeline caught 331 of 333 planted issues with no false flags. On an unseen dealer (a held-out test) whose problems I wrote afterward, it handled 32 of 87, and 23 bad listings got through, including a price of $3,489,500. Every new dealer feed needs its own quality gate.

What I built

The agent

  1. Understand. Turn one message into a structured intent: constraints, exclusions, preferences, context.
  2. Ask, don't guess. One clarifying question when the intent is vague or the results would be empty.
  3. Clean the data. Normalize and enrich dealer feeds so tow rating, range, features and body type are facts the agent can use.
  4. Find and explain. Filters and facets, then ranking, then a one-line "why this car" built only from listing facts.
  5. Prove it. These 20 queries become a gold set, scored on every change and published as a scoreboard.

PR/FAQ: RightFit

v0.7 · revised after testing · original v0.1 kept

Shoppers describe their life and their monthly budget, and see the cars that fit, each with a payment whose assumptions they control.

What the evidence changed since v0.1
  • Payment moved to the lead. 4 of 6 buyers couldn't work out a monthly payment online. v0.1 treated it as a bonus.
  • Ask about budget. Tuning to my own test taught the agent to ask fewer questions; real buyers wanted the budget question.
  • Every new data feed gets its own quality check. 331 of 333 planted problems caught, but only 32 of 87 on an unseen dealer.
  • Never loosen a filter silently. When nothing fits, show the trade-off and let the shopper choose.
Why the lender should build it

Payment is how most people actually budget. The lender is the one party that can show an honest, explained payment estimate next to every result, and it has a reason to care about trust and compliance from the start.

How success is measured
  • North star: findability rate, the share of sessions that reach a vehicle page matching every hard constraint the shopper stated.
  • Leading: zero-result rate, rephrase rate, time to first relevant result.
  • Quality: top-5 fit on a held-out set of real buyers' searches, asked the question the buyer wanted, filters nobody asked for and invented specs (target: zero for both).
  • Business: vehicle page to lead, and to credit application, against a control group.
  • Data: attribute completeness and accuracy per feed, listing freshness.
Guardrails
  • Ranking and payment estimates never use protected characteristics or proxies for them. The agent never asks about or infers income or credit, and a test checks that a shopper profile or dealer boost changes nothing.
  • Every applied filter shows as a chip the shopper can remove.
  • Explanations use listing facts only; automated tests check this on every change.
  • Quality bars are agreed with the business owner, and launch waits until they're met.

Method and limits

Each query was typed exactly as written into the site's public search box, and the applied filters, result count and top 5 were recorded. Top-5 fit was judged by the author against public specs; two queries that couldn't be verified are left out of the fit score. One main site, one day, one location, a small sample. Cars.com showed a bot check and wasn't tested. On Toyota.com's public new-inventory search, the entry point is a grid of models with no free-text box on that page. That's one observation of one public page, and it says nothing about any internal or upcoming product. All queries, raw results and scoring are in the repo.
